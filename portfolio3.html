1. Beer Taxes and Traffic Fatalities

During the 1980s, U.S. states pursued a range of strategies to reduce drunk driving. One potential tool is the taxation of alcohol: if beer is more expensive, less should be consumed — potentially saving lives. However, beer taxes vary across states for reasons that may be related to traffic fatality rates, such as revenue needs, political culture, and local attitudes toward alcohol regulation. In addition, states employed other policy levers during this period, including changes to the minimum legal drinking age and mandatory penalties for DUI offenses.

The data below is a panel of the 48 contiguous U.S. states, annually from 1982 through 1988. The traffic fatality rate is the number of traffic deaths per 10,000 population in a given state-year.

b)

A basic specification aimed at estimating the impact of beer taxes on traffic fatalities is:

fatality_rateit = \(\alpha\) + \(\delta\) * beertaxi + \(𝜂_i\)

Suppose that \(𝜂_{it}\) = \(\alpha_i\) + \(\alpha_t\) + \(\varepsilon_{it}\), where \(\alpha_i\) are state fixed effects and \(\alpha_t\) are year fixed effects.

\(𝛼_i\) may capture state fixed effects or things that are distinct to that specific state. The culture of the state might just be more geared towards beer consumption especially if there are universities in the area. Beer consumption tends to be higher in this case, regardless of beer tax. Climate can also be a factor; states that have heavier snowfall are more prone to traffic accidents.

\(𝛼_t\) may capture time fixed effects or things that may have evolved over time. Traffic safety regulations such as seat belt improvements or lowered highway speeds may have an effect on the fatality rate.

We assume that things that are trending across time are common across states, and that things that are specific to the state are the same over time. If one state is stricter toward traffic punishments (e.g. lowering the speed limit), this will confound the effect of beer tax toward fatality rate.

2. Retirement Savings and Crowding Out

Encouraging people to save for retirement is a major goal of policy makers. In this question, we investigate whether employer-sponsored savings plans crowd out private saving.

An Individual Retirement Account (IRA) is a tax-advantaged retirement savings account that individuals set up on their own. A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement plan. Policy makers have speculated that participation in a 401(k) might “crowd out” saving through other channels, such as an IRA. We ask: does participation in a 401(k) make it less likely that one will set up an IRA? Assume constant treatment effects.

The basic specification is: pirai = \(\alpha\) + \(\delta\) * p401ki + \(𝜂_i\)

a)

\(Y_{0i}\) - individual’s participation in IRA given that they have a 401k
\(Y_{1i}\) - individual’s non-participation in IRA given that they have a 401k

Individual treatment effect: \(Y_{0i}\) - \(Y_{1i}\) or 𝛿

b)

Omitted variable bias formula:
E(\(𝜂_i\) | \(D_i\), \(X_i\), \(A_i\)) = E(\(𝜂_i\) | \(X_i\), \(A_i\)) = \(X'_i\)𝛽 + 𝛾\(A_i\)
where \(A_i\) is the omitted variable or ability that we cannot observe (e.g. risk aversion)

If the individual is more risk averse or more likely to save, regardless of the treatment (opening a 401K account), it will bias the estimate upward. We might attribute the individual’s decision to open an IRA account due to the treatment, whereas they might just be more inclined to save.

d)

e401k must satisfy the relevance and exclusion conditions. Eligibility must be correlated with participation in 401k, which makes sense as you cannot participate in a 401k if you are not eligible. Eligibility should not be correlated to participation in IRA. This is less plausible than the first condition. Being employed in a company that offers a 401k might just make the individual more aware of the benefits of saving (i.e. if they are working for a financial company) and are therefore more inclined to save.
